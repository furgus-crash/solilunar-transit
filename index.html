Sun & Moon Transits Guide

1. Quick start

This app predicts when satellites will pass in front of the Sun or the Moon for the location and date you choose. It is built for public viewing sessions: you can see in advance which satellite will cross, exactly when, and where on the disc.

  • Set the observing location as precisely as you can (see section 2) and the viewing date.
  • Choose Sun or Moon as the target. The timeline shows only the hours it is above the horizon.
  • Wait for the status line under the display to say how many transits were found. The first time, the app downloads satellite data.
  • Drag the cyan Viewing Window on the timeline to match your session. The list shows every crossing in that window with start and end times.
  • Check the Next transit countdown at the top right during your session, and tap Preview next crossing to see where the next satellite will cross the disc and which way it will be heading.
  • Tap a row, or a Starlink train, to highlight it on the display.

2. Setting up

Location matters a lot. A satellite only crosses the Sun or Moon for people standing in a narrow strip of ground, typically a few kilometers to about 10 km wide. Moving a few kilometers can turn a transit into a near miss. The app starts at the Bell Museum (44.9914, −93.1881). Enter the exact spot where your telescopes will be, or tap Use GPS on site. Longitude is negative for west. If location access is blocked, a message appears and your coordinates stay as they were. Invalid numbers are rejected.

Viewing date. The date of your session. The app starts on today's date. Sunrise/Sunset (or Moonrise/Moonset) show when the target is up. All times use your phone's time zone.

Target. Sun or Moon. The Alt/Az box shows where it is at the start of your window. For the Moon it also shows how much of the disc is lit. If the Moon rises and sets twice within your date, a menu appears so you can choose which period to use.

3. Satellite data

Orbits come from CelesTrak's list of active satellites, about 15,000 including all Starlink, the ISS, China's space station and Hubble. The list is saved on your phone, and every calculation runs on your phone. It is shared with the deep-sky app, so a download in one is used by the other.

  • Update data downloads a fresh list. The app also refreshes automatically when the saved copy is over 12 hours old. For the best transit accuracy, tap Update data on the day of your session (an ISS reboost or a Starlink maneuver makes older data wrong).
  • CelesTrak allows about one download per internet connection every 2 hours. If you see "CelesTrak refused", wait, use mobile data, or use Load file with a satellite list saved from CelesTrak.
  • The status line warns you if your date is more than 2 days from the orbit data.

4. The display

The display shows the Sun or Moon as a large disc. The dotted ring marks the central half of the disc. Up on the screen means toward the sky overhead (the zenith) and right means increasing azimuth. In a telescope the picture may be flipped or rotated, so follow the disc and the path rather than left and right.

Each line is a satellite's path across the disc, with an arrowhead showing the direction of travel. Satellites do not need to be sunlit to transit: you see their silhouette against the disc. Names are not drawn on the picture. Tap a row in the list to identify a satellite.

  • Central Transit: crosses the inner half of the disc (within half the radius of the center). Best views.
  • Edge Transit: crosses the disc but nearer the edge. Orbit predictions have some uncertainty, so one very close to the rim could miss.

The two Show buttons hide or show each kind on both the display and the list.

5. Timeline and viewing window

The timeline covers only the time the Sun or Moon is above the horizon on your date. The colored lines are every transit all through that period, so you can see busy and quiet times at a glance. The ends of the bar are labeled with the rise and set times (sunrise and sunset, or moonrise and moonset) and whole hours are marked in between, so you always know where your window sits in the day.

  • Move the cyan window by dragging its middle. Resize it with the ║ handles (minimum 10 minutes). Tap an empty spot on the bar to center the window there.
  • The display, the list, and the counts update instantly for the window you choose.
  • Reset puts the window back to one hour: starting now if you are looking at today and the target is up, otherwise in the middle of the period.

6. Predicted transits list

The list shows every pass in your window, earliest first. Start and End are the moments the satellite enters and leaves the disc, in local time to a tenth of a second. Most transits last only 0.5 to 2 seconds.

Tap a row to select it. Its trail on the display turns dotted and an info panel opens at the bottom with: start and end times, duration, distance, how close it comes to the center of the disc, how fast it moves across the sky, its altitude above your horizon, and its orbit (height, speed, inclination, NORAD ID). Tap the row again, or Close, to clear it. The selection also clears if you move the window so it leaves.

7. ISS, China's station, Hubble and Starlink trains

★ Special satellites: the ISS, the Chinese Space Station (CSS) and the Hubble Space Telescope are marked with a green ★, drawn with a thicker line, and labeled on the display. Modules and visiting spacecraft docked to a station share its position, so they are merged into a single entry, with the other names listed in the info panel.

Starlink trains: when three or more Starlink satellites from the same launch cross within 30 seconds of each other, they are grouped as a train. A cyan banner above the list gives the number of satellites, the time span and how many actually cross the disc. Tap it to highlight every member on the display and open a summary. Members also carry a green TRAIN tag in the list. New launches appear as tight trains in the first days after launch, so refresh the data on the day.

8. Next transit countdown

At the top right, a live countdown shows the next crossing after the current time (not just the ones in your window): what it is, how central it is, the exact time and how long to wait. It turns green in the last 2 minutes so you can point it out to the public. Below it, a green Next highlight line shows the next ISS, Chinese station, Hubble or Starlink train, which are the transits most worth announcing. If you pick another date, it counts down to the first crossing after the current time on that date.

Preview next crossing. Tap this button under the countdown to show only that crossing on the display: a thick line with a white dot where it enters the disc and an arrowhead showing where it is heading, plus a description in plain words (for example "enters at the 8 o'clock edge and leaves near 2 o'clock, moving up and to the right"). It moves on to the following crossing automatically after each one. For a Starlink train, all its satellites are shown. Tap Back to window view to return, or tap a row in the list, which also ends the preview.

9. Safety

Never look at the Sun, directly or through a telescope, binoculars or camera, without a certified solar filter correctly fitted. A transit lasts only a second or two, so don't take a filter off or remove your eye protection to try to see it. Moon viewing needs no filter.

10. How accurate is it?

Calculated from real data: satellite positions (the standard SGP4 model with CelesTrak's orbit data), the Sun's and Moon's real positions (the Moon includes the shift from where you stand, up to about 1°), and the exact moments the satellite crosses the disc. In tests, the calculation timed a crossing placed at the center of the Sun and of the Moon to within a few thousandths of a second.

What limits it in practice: orbit data. With fresh data (a day old or less), a satellite's position is typically good to a kilometer or two. That shifts its path by roughly a tenth of a degree (about a fifth of the Sun's or Moon's width) and its timing by a second or less. So treat Central transits as likely, Edge transits as less certain (one near the rim can miss), and expect timing within about a second or two. Older data, satellite maneuvers, and your exact position make this worse. The predictions have not been compared with live observed transits yet, so compare them with another tracker if you can.

Not included: satellites missing from the public list (some military or brand-new ones), aircraft, birds, and atmospheric refraction (small except near the horizon). High-orbit satellites (orbits over about 225 minutes) are skipped because they barely move across the sky.

11. Using it at a session

  • Open the page once with a connection before the event so the app and the data are saved. Then it opens and calculates without signal. (Phone GPS usually works without signal too.)
  • On the day, tap Update data, confirm the location, then set the window for your session.
  • Add it to your Home Screen: in Safari tap Share, then Add to Home Screen.
  • After the app is updated on GitHub, open it twice to see the new version.

12. Troubleshooting

  • "CelesTrak refused the download": the 2-hour limit. Wait, use mobile data, or Load file.
  • "Could not download satellite data": no connection and no saved copy yet. Connect once.
  • "The Sun/Moon is not above the horizon on this date": pick another date.
  • No transits: normal! Most windows have none. Look at the timeline for busier times, or widen the window.
  • "(could not refresh, using saved copy)": the refresh failed, so older saved data is being used.
  • Predictions differ from another site: check the data date, your exact location, and the time zone.
