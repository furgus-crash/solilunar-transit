Sun & Moon Transits Guide

1. Quick start

This app predicts when satellites will pass in front of the Sun or the Moon for the location and date you choose. It is built for public viewing sessions: you can see in advance which satellite will cross, exactly when, and where on the disc.

  • Set the observing location as precisely as you can (see section 2) and the viewing date.
  • Choose Sun or Moon as the target. The timeline shows only the hours it is above the horizon.
  • Wait for the status line under the display to say how many transits were found. The first time, the app downloads satellite data.
  • Drag the cyan Viewing Window on the timeline to match your session. The list shows every crossing in that window with start and end times.
  • Check the Next transit countdown at the top right during your session, choose which kind of transit to wait for (any satellite, the ISS, the CSS or Hubble), and tap Preview next crossing to see exactly where it will cross the disc and which way it will be heading. Turn on Beeps for a 3-2-1 audio countdown.
  • Tap a row, or a Starlink train, to highlight it on the display.

2. Setting up

Location matters a lot. A satellite only crosses the Sun or Moon for people standing in a narrow strip of ground, typically a few kilometers to about 10 km wide. Moving a few kilometers can turn a transit into a near miss. The app starts at the Bell Museum (44.9914, −93.1881). Enter the exact spot where your telescopes will be, tap Use GPS on site, or tap Map to choose it on a map: drag the map, tap to drop the pin, zoom in close with + and − (or pinch), then tap Use this location. The map needs an internet connection (it shows OpenStreetMap), but coordinates and GPS work without one. Longitude is negative for west. If location access is blocked, a message appears and your coordinates stay as they were. Invalid numbers are rejected.

Viewing date. The date of your session. The app starts on today's date. Sunrise/Sunset (or Moonrise/Moonset) show when the target is up. All times use your phone's time zone.

Target. Sun or Moon. The Alt/Az box shows where it is at the start of your window. For the Moon it also shows how much of the disc is lit. If the Moon rises and sets twice within your date, a menu appears so you can choose which period to use.

3. Satellite data

Orbits come from CelesTrak's list of active satellites, about 15,000 including all Starlink, the ISS, China's space station and Hubble. The list is saved on your phone, and every calculation runs on your phone. It is shared with the deep-sky app, so a download in one is used by the other.

  • Update data downloads a fresh list. The app also refreshes automatically when the saved copy is over 12 hours old. For the best transit accuracy, tap Update data on the day of your session (an ISS reboost or a Starlink maneuver makes older data wrong).
  • CelesTrak allows about one download per internet connection every 2 hours. If you see "CelesTrak refused", wait, use mobile data, or use Load file with a satellite list saved from CelesTrak.
  • The status line warns you if your date is more than 2 days from the orbit data.

4. The display

The display shows the Sun or Moon as a large disc. The dotted ring marks the central half of the disc. Up on the screen means toward the sky overhead (the zenith) and right means increasing azimuth. In a telescope the picture may be flipped or rotated, so follow the disc and the path rather than left and right.

Each line is a satellite's path across the disc, with an arrowhead showing the direction of travel. Satellites do not need to be sunlit to transit: you see their silhouette against the disc. Names are not drawn on the picture. Tap a row in the list to identify a satellite.

  • Central Transit: crosses the inner half of the disc (within half the radius of the center). Best views.
  • Edge Transit: crosses the disc but nearer the edge. Orbit predictions have some uncertainty, so one very close to the rim could miss.

The two Show buttons hide or show each kind on both the display and the list.

5. Sunspot view

When the Sun is the target, tap Show sunspots above the picture to turn the display into a map of the sunspot groups on the Sun right now. Tap Back to transit view to return. This button is hidden when the Moon is selected.

  • What you see: the disc, drawn the same way up as the satellite view, with faint grid lines every 30° of solar latitude and longitude. A cyan N marks the direction of the Sun's north pole. Each group is circled and labeled with its NOAA number. Groups are drawn to true size, and a tiny blue Earth at the same scale sits in the bottom-left corner.
  • Tap a group for details, and tap it again (or tap elsewhere, or tap the details card) to close them.
  • Where it comes from: NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center publishes a daily list of sunspot groups with positions as of 00:00 UTC. The app moves each group forward to the current time using the Sun's rotation (about 13° a day), so groups drift toward the western edge during the day, and ones that rotated past it disappear. Groups that appeared after the daily report are not shown, and the individual spots inside a group are not drawn.
  • A telescope image may be flipped or rotated compared with the screen, so use the N marker and the pattern of groups to match them up. Never look at the Sun without a certified solar filter.
  • The first time it needs an internet connection. After that, the last list it downloaded is saved and used offline (the status line shows how old it is).

What the details mean

  • Sunspot group vs. spot: a single sunspot is one dark spot. Spots form in clusters, and NOAA gives each cluster one number (an "active region", written AR followed by the number). The ring on the display is the whole group. "Individual spots" is how many dark spots are inside it.
  • Plage region (no spots): sometimes NOAA numbers an area that has no sunspots at all, only a bright magnetic patch called a plage. It shows as a dashed ring. You cannot see plages through a normal white-light solar filter, only with hydrogen-alpha or calcium filters, so don't expect to find one in your telescope.
  • Location (for example S15W32): the group's place on the Sun: 15° south of the Sun's equator and 32° west of the center line of the disc, as measured when NOAA counted it. N means north, S south, E east and W west (as seen from Earth).
  • Area, compared with Earth: how much surface the group covers compared with a flat circle the size of the Earth. "About 1 Earth" means the same area as Earth. "12 Earths" means twelve times that area. "20% of an Earth" means a fifth. "Too small to measure" means NOAA reports almost no area. NOAA measures area in "millionths of the Sun's visible half" (MSH); about 42 of those equal one Earth.
  • Length: how far the group stretches along the Sun's surface, in degrees of solar longitude. One degree is about 12,100 km (7,500 miles), so a 15° group is about 182,000 km, or roughly 14 Earths lined up end to end.
  • Sunspot class (three letters, for example Ekc): the McIntosh code. First letter: the type of group (A or B, small and simple; C to F, pairs of spots that get longer and more developed; H, a single big spot). Second letter: the fringe (penumbra) of the biggest spot (x none, r rudimentary, s small round, a small uneven, h large round, k large uneven). Third letter: how packed the spots are (x single spot, o spread out, i moderately packed, c tightly packed).
  • Magnetic type: Alpha: one magnetic pole, simple and quiet. Beta: the usual two poles. Beta-Gamma: tangled poles, flares more likely. Delta (for example Beta-Gamma-Delta): opposite poles jammed together, the most flare-prone. Sunspots are strongly magnetic, and these types describe how complicated that magnetism is.

6. Timeline and viewing window

The timeline covers only the time the Sun or Moon is above the horizon on your date. The colored lines are every transit all through that period, so you can see busy and quiet times at a glance. The ends of the bar are labeled with the rise and set times (sunrise and sunset, or moonrise and moonset) and whole hours are marked in between, so you always know where your window sits in the day.

  • Move the cyan window by dragging its middle. Resize it with the ║ handles (minimum 10 minutes). Tap an empty spot on the bar to center the window there.
  • The display, the list, and the counts update instantly for the window you choose.
  • Shortcuts: tap the cyan window once to jump to the current time with a 60-minute window, wherever the window is and however long it is. Double-tap it to stretch it over the whole period from rise to set (sunrise to sunset, or moonrise to moonset).
  • Reset puts the window back to one hour: starting now if you are looking at today and the target is up, otherwise in the middle of the period.

7. Predicted transits list

The list shows every pass in your window, earliest first. Start and End are the moments the satellite enters and leaves the disc, in local time to a tenth of a second. Most transits last only 0.5 to 2 seconds.

Tap a row to select it. Its trail on the display turns dotted and an info panel opens at the bottom with: start and end times, duration, distance, how close it comes to the center of the disc, how fast it moves across the sky, its altitude above your horizon, and its orbit (height, speed, inclination, NORAD ID). Tap the row again, or Close, to clear it. The selection also clears if you move the window so it leaves.

8. ISS, China's station, Hubble and Starlink trains

★ Special satellites: the ISS, the Chinese Space Station (CSS) and the Hubble Space Telescope are marked with a green ★, drawn with a thicker line, and labeled on the display. Modules and visiting spacecraft docked to a station share its position, so they are merged into a single entry, with the other names listed in the info panel.

Starlink trains: when three or more Starlink satellites from the same launch cross within 30 seconds of each other, they are grouped as a train. A cyan banner above the list gives the number of satellites, the time span and how many actually cross the disc. Tap it to highlight every member on the display and open a summary. Members also carry a green TRAIN tag in the list. New launches appear as tight trains in the first days after launch, so refresh the data on the day.

9. Which satellites are easiest to see

Satellites are tiny compared with the Sun and Moon, so only the biggest show any shape in a telescope. How big a satellite looks depends on its real size and how far away it is. In the list, the largest ones carry a size line such as "109 m (357 ft) across, looks about 45″ wide (2.3% of the Sun)". That is the satellite's size divided by its distance from you, written in arcseconds (the Sun and Moon are each roughly 1,800 to 1,900 arcseconds wide). The Next transit card also tells you when the next one is a satellite from this list. From easiest to hardest:

  • International Space Station (ISS): about 109 m (357 ft) across including its solar arrays, and 73 m (240 ft) the other way. The easiest satellite to see in front of the Sun. It orbits low (about 420 km), so on a close pass it can look over 60 arcseconds wide, with a distinct, recognizable silhouette.
  • Tiangong, the Chinese Space Station (CSS): about 55 m (180 ft) long. Smaller than the ISS but still far bigger than any ordinary satellite.
  • Hubble Space Telescope (HST): about 13 m (43 ft) long and 4 m (14 ft) wide. A distinct target, but much smaller than a space station. It orbits a little higher (about 520 km), so it is smaller and quicker. Because its orbit stays within 28.5° of the equator, transits seen from northern places like Minnesota are uncommon.
  • Newer Starlinks (V2 Mini and Direct to Cell): their big solar arrays make them about 30 m (100 ft) across, large enough that you might notice a shape. Older Starlinks are only about 11 m (36 ft) end to end and look like a tiny speck. The app tells them apart by launch year (2024 or later is the larger type, 2022 or earlier the older type, and 2023 could be either), so treat that label as a good guess.

These sizes are the satellite's longest dimension. How wide it looks in the telescope also depends on which way it is turned. All other satellites are small, so expect only a dark dot or a short streak.

10. Next transit, preview and beeps

The Next transit card, at the top of the page, looks ahead from the current time (not just your window). Use the menu to choose what to wait for: Next satellite transit (any), Next ISS transit, Next CSS transit or Next Hubble transit. It shows the satellite, how central the crossing is, the exact start and end times to a tenth of a second, and a live countdown. It turns green in the last 2 minutes. For any satellite, a green Next highlight line also shows the next ISS, CSS, Hubble or Starlink train. In the last 10 seconds a big countdown appears, and it says TRANSIT NOW while the crossing is happening.

Preview next crossing. Shows only the transit you chose, drawn in green on the disc: a thick line, a white dot where it enters, a white ring where it leaves, an arrowhead for the direction, and the exact START and END times in the corners. Under the button you also get a plain-words description (for example "enters at the 3 o'clock edge and leaves near 9 o'clock, moving left"). It moves on to the following crossing by itself after each one. For a Starlink train all its satellites are shown. Tap Back to window view to return, or tap a row in the list, which also ends the preview.

Beeps. Tap Beeps: off to turn sound on (you will hear a short test beep). For the transit you chose, the app beeps at 3, 2 and 1 second before the start, then plays a steady higher tone for exactly as long as the transit lasts, so you can keep your eye on the telescope and listen. Tips: raise the volume and switch off the iPhone's silent mode; keep the page open (the app asks the phone to keep the screen on while beeps are on); set the phone's date and time to automatic so its clock is accurate. Predictions can be off by a second or two, so be watching a couple of seconds before the first beep.

11. Safety

Never look at the Sun, directly or through a telescope, binoculars or camera, without a certified solar filter correctly fitted. A transit lasts only a second or two, so don't take a filter off or remove your eye protection to try to see it. Moon viewing needs no filter.

12. How accurate is it?

Calculated from real data: satellite positions (the standard SGP4 model with CelesTrak's orbit data), the Sun's and Moon's real positions (the Moon includes the shift from where you stand, up to about 1°), and the exact moments the satellite crosses the disc. In tests, the calculation timed a crossing placed at the center of the Sun and of the Moon to within a few thousandths of a second.

What limits it in practice: orbit data. With fresh data (a day old or less), a satellite's position is typically good to a kilometer or two. That shifts its path by roughly a tenth of a degree (about a fifth of the Sun's or Moon's width) and its timing by a second or less. So treat Central transits as likely, Edge transits as less certain (one near the rim can miss), and expect timing within about a second or two. Older data, satellite maneuvers, and your exact position make this worse. The predictions have not been compared with live observed transits yet, so compare them with another tracker if you can.

Not included: satellites missing from the public list (some military or brand-new ones), aircraft, birds, and atmospheric refraction (small except near the horizon). High-orbit satellites (orbits over about 225 minutes) are skipped because they barely move across the sky.

13. Using it at a session

  • Open the page once with a connection before the event so the app and the data are saved. Then it opens and calculates without signal. (Phone GPS usually works without signal too.)
  • On the day, tap Update data, confirm the location, then set the window for your session.
  • When a crossing you want is coming up, choose its type in the Next transit card, turn on Beeps, and use the preview to tell the audience where to look.
  • Add it to your Home Screen: in Safari tap Share, then Add to Home Screen.
  • After the app is updated on GitHub, open it twice to see the new version.

14. Troubleshooting

  • "CelesTrak refused the download": the 2-hour limit. Wait, use mobile data, or Load file.
  • "Could not download satellite data": no connection and no saved copy yet. Connect once.
  • "The Sun/Moon is not above the horizon on this date": pick another date.
  • "Could not load sunspot data": NOAA's site could not be reached. Check the connection and tap the button again. A saved copy is used if there is one.
  • The map is blank: it needs an internet connection. Type coordinates or use GPS instead.
  • No transits: normal! Most windows have none. Look at the timeline for busier times, or widen the window.
  • No beeps: tap Beeps so it says "on", raise the volume, turn off silent mode, and keep the screen on. Beeps only play when a transit of the chosen type is about to start.
  • "(could not refresh, using saved copy)": the refresh failed, so older saved data is being used.
  • Predictions differ from another site: check the data date, your exact location, and the time zone.
